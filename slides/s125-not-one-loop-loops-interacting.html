I · What a business actually is

Not one loop. Loops interacting.

Systems · Constraints · Agents

No single loop explains the behaviour. What you observe is whichever loop currently dominates — and dominance shifts.

Growth loop (R)

Win work → revenue → capacity to win more work.

Left alone it compounds. It is also the loop everyone is measured on.

Load loop (B)

More work → the constrained station saturates → quality and lead time degrade → less work won.

Quiet for a long time, then suddenly the only thing that matters.

Erosion loop (B, delayed)

Pressure → shortcuts → rework, attrition, vendor goodwill spent → less capacity than before.

This is the one that lowers the ceiling.

Shifting dominance

Every shape on the behaviour slide is a story about which loop is winning. An S-curve is not one loop — it is a reinforcing loop handing dominance to a balancing one. Overshoot is the same handover arriving too late.

Your constraint is the balancing loop that currently dominates. Find the dominant loop and you have found the constraint — they are one object with two names.

This slide is the bridge from systems thinking into Theory of Constraints, and it is worth slowing down for.

The closing line is the unification. Systems dynamics says 'the dominant balancing loop governs the behaviour'. Goldratt says 'the constraint governs the throughput'. These are the same claim in two vocabularies, arrived at independently. Pods who see that stop treating the two halves of Day 1 as separate subjects.

The practical consequence: dominance shifts, so the constraint moves. That is step 5 of the focusing steps, and this is why it exists.

If the room is quiet, ask which of the three loops their own organisation measures. It is almost always the growth loop, and almost never the erosion loop — which is precisely why the erosion loop is where the damage accumulates unseen.
